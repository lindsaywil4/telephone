Ask 1 Engineered speed control

People will drive whatever speed the street design invites. Signs and paint won't fix a four-lane cross-section; vertical elements will.

Raised crossings at Lockwood and at the Dumble curve

Bookend the busiest school blocks with raised crossings (or a raised intersection where geometry allows) so the whole segment between them self-enforces the speed limit. The Dumble curve is one block from Eastwood Academy and drew the largest cluster of pedestrian-signal pins in our poll.

Why it's realistic: the city is installing raised crossings on the Navigation Greenway project right now, and a raised school crossing already works at Woodhead @ Hawthorne. With Telephone's storm system being rebuilt anyway, tabled crossings create natural high points and inlets can be placed wherever needed. Fire access: departments generally prefer raised-crossing profiles over humps (roughly 3 seconds per device for an engine), and the design should be worked with Station 18, which is on this corridor. Everyone at the speed limit — including fire trucks — is the goal; what actually delays a response is a crash-caused jam.

Ask 2 Crossings where people actually cross

Crossing spacing on paper is not crossing behavior on the ground. Our poll and daily observation show exactly where people go.

New crossing at Eddington: refuge island + RRFB

The plan currently shows no crossing where neighbors cross daily to Tlaquepaque Market and Bohemeo's — the corridor's main destination and the reason this segment ranked so high in the livable centers work. Ask: a marked crossing with a pedestrian refuge island in the existing painted median area, plus a rectangular rapid flashing beacon.

Why it's realistic: the island is striping and ramps — design hours, not budget lines. An RRFB runs roughly $10–15k (a rounding error in this project) pending a standard warrant check; RRFBs were added to Navigation Greenway on community request and the city installed them in the Heights this summer. A refuge matters most to the slowest crossers: small kids, elders, and the ~1 in 4 Greater Eastwood households traveling without a car.

RRFBs at the other student crossings

Lockwood, Pease/Monroe, Jefferson/Wesley, and Lawndale line up with school desire lines for Navarro, Eastwood Academy, Austin High, and the three-school KIPP campus. Where a full signal isn't warranted, an RRFB raises driver yielding at low cost.

Ask 3 Shade that survives

The poll's worst-rated element was the buffer between sidewalk and road; shade trees were the most-requested feature. These are the same fix.

True shade species, engineered to thrive in a 4-ft buffer

Swap ornamental hollies for shade canopy: cedar elm, live oak, Mexican sycamore, bur oak, Monterrey oak — planted along the corridor and the school spurs on Dumble and Lawndale. Mature canopy doubles as traffic calming by visually narrowing the road, and a tree in the Eddington refuge island marks the crossing.

Why it's realistic: four feet is workable when the subgrade is designed for it — root-guidance structures so roots grow down instead of buckling pavement, and self-watering subgrade cells as used on Shepherd/Durham where no management district maintains irrigation. Two asks that cost little but decide everything: name the landscape architect on the project, and show the watering/maintenance budget for the establishment years — the Montrose Blvd plantings show what happens when beautiful trees go in without one.

For the record Questions for the design team

We're not proposing solutions to all of these — some are simply items where the drawings raised questions we'd like answered, and answered on record.

  1. HAWKs at Stimson and Claremont/Fourcade: both sit at skewed intersections. How will the beacons and markings read unmistakably as pedestrian crossings on the diagonal — and how is the side street handled, given that side-street drivers at intersection HAWKs commonly misread stopped mainline traffic as their gap and pull into the crosswalk?
  2. Broadmoor / Baird at Telephone–Lawndale: converting Broadmoor to stop control while Telephone's left-turn queue for Lawndale blocks the path leaves Broadmoor drivers with one or two car-lengths of storage and no protected movement. As drawn this still looks like a conflict point — can it get a second look?
  3. Lane widths: are all lanes 11 ft? Shepherd/Durham compromised to a 10/11 mix; every foot recovered is buffer and tree space.
  4. Speed limit: the corridor precedent exists for posting 30 instead of 35. Is that in scope here?
  5. Shared-use path vs. buffer: in the pinch points, the path sits against live traffic with no planting room. If the practical difference is about two feet, the community should get to weigh that trade-off explicitly — wider path now, or buffer and trees that make the whole corridor walkable — through a public comment window before curbs are locked in.
  6. Crossing spacing: the spacing standard was written for corridors with miles between crossings, not a main street with eight schools and continuous destinations. Will crossing placement follow observed desire lines here?
The frame for all of it: this project was ranked a top livable-centers priority because it connects the neighborhood to its own main street. A design is succeeding when a kid can walk to school, an elder can reach the market, and a fire engine can roll from Station 18 — all on the same street, all at the speed limit.
